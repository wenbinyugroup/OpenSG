Theory#

OpenSG implements the linear elastic form of the Mechanics of Structure Genome (MSG). Its solid engine and shell engine share the theory below. The tutorials show the input files. The OpenSG 2.x documentation describes the code.

Notation: \(\epsilon\) is the small parameter and \(\varepsilon\) a strain. \(\langle\cdot\rangle\) is the average and \(\langle\langle\cdot\rangle\rangle\) the integral over the SG.

Structure gene and macroscopic model#

MSG builds the constitutive law of a macroscopic model out of the original heterogeneous structure (Yu 2025, Sec. 1.11). In the solid engine the original model is 3-D linear elasticity (Bagla et al. 2026, Sec. 1), and in the shell engine a shell model of the walls (Deo and Yu 2020, and Bagla et al. 2025a).

The Structure Gene (SG) is the smallest mathematical building block of the structure, and its dimension follows the heterogeneity and the macroscopic model (Yu 2025, Sec. 1.11):

  • 1-D: a line through the thickness of a laminate, for a plate or a 3-D solid,

  • 2-D: a beam cross-section, a plate cell heterogeneous in one in-plane direction, or a fiber composite modeled as a 3-D solid,

  • 3-D: a block or unit cell, for a beam heterogeneous along its span, a plate heterogeneous in both in-plane directions, or a 3-D solid.

The unknown fields of the SG follow from the principle of minimum information loss: the energies of the original and the macroscopic model should differ as little as possible (Yu 2025, Sec. 1.11). The variational asymptotic method (VAM) drops the small terms of that energy, because the SG is much smaller than the wavelength of the macroscopic deformation (Yu 2025, Secs. 1.11 and 7.2). Homogenization solves the problem over the SG for the effective law of the macroscopic model. Dehomogenization turns the macroscopic response into the local displacement, strain and stress inside the SG (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.3).

The macro strains \(\bar\varepsilon\) carry engineering shears, with the factor 2:

  • 3-D solid: \([\varepsilon_{11}\ \varepsilon_{22}\ \varepsilon_{33}\ 2\varepsilon_{23}\ 2\varepsilon_{13}\ 2\varepsilon_{12}]^T\) (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.4).

  • Kirchhoff-Love plate: the in-plane strains and the curvatures, \([\varepsilon_{11}\ \varepsilon_{22}\ 2\varepsilon_{12}\ \kappa_{11}\ \kappa_{22}\ 2\kappa_{12}]^T\) (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.4). The Reissner-Mindlin plate adds the transverse shear strains \(\gamma = [2\gamma_{13}\ 2\gamma_{23}]^T\) (Yu et al. 2003).

  • Euler-Bernoulli beam: \([\gamma_{11}\ \kappa_1\ \kappa_2\ \kappa_3]^T\) (Bagla et al. 2025a, Eq. 15).

  • Timoshenko beam: \([\gamma_{11}\ 2\gamma_{12}\ 2\gamma_{13}\ \kappa_1\ \kappa_2\ \kappa_3]^T\) (Yu et al. 2012, Eq. 113).

The beam order is 1 extension, 2 and 3 transverse shear, 4 twist, 5 and 6 bending, and it pairs with the forces \([F_1\ F_2\ F_3\ M_1\ M_2\ M_3]\) (Yu et al. 2012, Eq. 113). The 3-D solid, the Kirchhoff-Love plate and the Euler-Bernoulli beam are the classical models, the Reissner-Mindlin plate and the Timoshenko beam the refined ones (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.3).

Kinematics of the SG#

A beam keeps the macro coordinate \(x_1\) along its reference line, with \(x_2, x_3\) in the cross-section. A plate keeps \(x_1, x_2\), and a 3-D solid all three. The SG uses the micro coordinates \(y_i = x_i/\epsilon\) (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.2): \(y_3\) for a 1-D SG, \(y_2, y_3\) for a 2-D SG and all three for a 3-D SG.

The displacement inside the SG is that of the macroscopic model plus a fluctuation \(\epsilon w_i\). For the 3-D solid (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.4)

\[ u_i(x, y) = \bar u_i(x) + \epsilon\, w_i(x, y), \]

and the plate and the beam add the rotations of their reference surface or line (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.4). The local strain \(\Gamma = [\Gamma_{11}\ \Gamma_{22}\ \Gamma_{33}\ 2\Gamma_{23}\ 2\Gamma_{13}\ 2\Gamma_{12}]^T\) splits into a fluctuation part, with \(w = [w_1\ w_2\ w_3]^T\), and a macroscopic part. For a prismatic beam, a plate or a 3-D solid, which carry no initial twist or curvature (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.2),

\[ \Gamma = \Gamma_h w + \Gamma_\varepsilon \bar\varepsilon + \epsilon\, \Gamma_l w' . \]

For a 3-D SG, \(\Gamma_h\) holds the derivatives with respect to \(y_i\) (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.2):

\[\begin{split} \Gamma_h = \begin{bmatrix} \partial_1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \partial_2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \partial_3 \\ 0 & \partial_3 & \partial_2 \\ \partial_3 & 0 & \partial_1 \\ \partial_2 & \partial_1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \partial_i = \frac{\partial}{\partial y_i} . \end{split}\]

A 2-D SG drops the \(\partial_1\) terms, and a 1-D SG also drops the \(\partial_2\) terms. \(\Gamma_\varepsilon\) places the macro strains in the SG and is the identity for the 3-D solid. For the beam, with \(x_\alpha = \epsilon y_\alpha\) and \(\alpha = 2, 3\), it gives \(\Gamma_{11} = \gamma_{11} + x_3\kappa_2 - x_2\kappa_3\), \(2\Gamma_{12} = -x_3\kappa_1\) and \(2\Gamma_{13} = x_2\kappa_1\) (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.2). The last term holds the derivatives along the macro coordinates, \(w' = \partial w/\partial x_1\) for a beam, and enters only the first-order step of the refined models.

The fluctuations are constrained so that the macroscopic displacement is the average displacement of the SG, and a beam adds a fourth constraint, which defines its twist (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.2):

\[ \langle w_i \rangle = 0, \qquad \langle w_{2|3} - w_{3|2} \rangle = 0 , \]

where \((\cdot)_{|j}\) is the derivative with respect to \(y_j\). When the SG is periodic in a direction, \(w\) takes equal values on the matching boundaries (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.4, and Bagla et al. 2026, Sec. 3.3).

Homogenization#

The strain energy density of the macroscopic model is (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.3)

\[ \frac{1}{2\omega} \left\langle\!\left\langle \Gamma^T C\, \Gamma \right\rangle\!\right\rangle , \]

with \(C\) the \(6\times6\) stiffness of the material at each point. \(\omega\) is the size of the SG along the macro coordinates that the model keeps, and 1 when the SG has none of them (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.3), as for a beam cross-section or a laminate under a plate. For a 3-D solid it is the length, area or volume that the SG spans, voids included.

VAM keeps the leading terms of this energy, and the fluctuation solves the zeroth-order problem (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.3)

\[ \min_{w} \ \left\langle\!\left\langle \left(\Gamma_h w + \Gamma_\varepsilon \bar\varepsilon\right)^T C \left(\Gamma_h w + \Gamma_\varepsilon \bar\varepsilon\right) \right\rangle\!\right\rangle \]

under the constraints, with \(\bar\varepsilon\) held fixed. OpenSG solves it with finite elements over the SG mesh, \(w = S V\) with the shape functions \(S\) and the nodal values \(V\). Twice the strain energy \(U\) stored in the SG, \(\omega\) times the density above, is (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.3)

\[ 2U = V^T E\, V + 2 V^T D_{h\varepsilon} \bar\varepsilon + \bar\varepsilon^T D_{\varepsilon\varepsilon} \bar\varepsilon, \]

with

\[\begin{split} \begin{aligned} E &= \left\langle\!\left\langle (\Gamma_h S)^T C\, (\Gamma_h S) \right\rangle\!\right\rangle, \\ D_{h\varepsilon} &= \left\langle\!\left\langle (\Gamma_h S)^T C\, \Gamma_\varepsilon \right\rangle\!\right\rangle, \\ D_{\varepsilon\varepsilon} &= \left\langle\!\left\langle \Gamma_\varepsilon^T C\, \Gamma_\varepsilon \right\rangle\!\right\rangle . \end{aligned} \end{split}\]

The minimum under the constraints is one linear system with one right-hand side per macro strain (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.3):

\[ E\, V_0 = -D_{h\varepsilon}, \qquad V = V_0\, \bar\varepsilon . \]

Substituting \(V\) back gives the effective stiffness of the macroscopic model (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.3):

\[ \bar C = \frac{1}{\omega} \left( D_{\varepsilon\varepsilon} + V_0^T D_{h\varepsilon} \right). \]

The classical laws come from this zeroth-order problem (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.3).

Refined models#

The refined laws need one more order.

Timoshenko beam. The fluctuation gets a first-order part, \(V = V_0\bar\varepsilon + V_{1S}\,\bar\varepsilon'\), where \(\bar\varepsilon = [\gamma_{11}\ \kappa_1\ \kappa_2\ \kappa_3]^T\) are the classical strains, \(\bar\varepsilon'\) their derivative along \(x_1\), and \(V_{1S}\) solves a second system with the same matrix \(E\) (Yu 2025, Sec. 6.3). The Timoshenko strains enter through \(\bar\varepsilon = \bar\varepsilon_t + Q\gamma'\), with \(Q\) a constant \(4\times2\) matrix (Yu 2025, Sec. 6.3). Here \(\bar\varepsilon_t\) holds the extension, twist and bending strains measured in the Timoshenko model, and \(\gamma = [2\gamma_{12}\ 2\gamma_{13}]^T\) the transverse shear strains (Yu et al. 2012, Eq. 113). The beam equilibrium equations remove the derivatives from the energy, now correct to the second order (Yu 2025, Sec. 6.3):

\[ 2U = \bar\varepsilon_t^T X\, \bar\varepsilon_t + 2\, \bar\varepsilon_t^T Y \gamma + \gamma^T G_s\, \gamma , \]

where \(X\), \(Y\) and \(G_s\) are the \(4\times4\), \(4\times2\) and \(2\times2\) blocks of the Timoshenko \(6\times6\), reordered into the beam order.

Reissner-Mindlin plate. The energy correct to the second order is brought to the Reissner-Mindlin form with the least loss of energy, so no shear correction factor is used (Yu 2005, and Yu 2025, Sec. 5.6). OpenSG fits the shear block \(G\) by the least-squares construction of Yu et al. (2002, 2003). Yu (2005) later refined this fit. The least-squares construction is for a laminate, a 1-D SG. OpenSG also applies it to 2-D and 3-D SGs, a route not yet validated for SGs heterogeneous in plane.

The two engines#

Solid engine#

The solid engine meshes the SG with solid elements: line, area or volume elements for a 1-D, 2-D or 3-D SG. Its SG problem keeps one form for every macroscopic model (Bagla et al. 2025b, Sec. 4). \(\Gamma_\varepsilon\), \(\omega\) and the constraints change with the model (Yu 2025, Secs. 7.2 and 7.3). The engine returns every law of the table and, for a beam cross-section (2-D SG), a \(6\times6\) mass matrix built from the density \(\rho\) (Bagla et al. 2025b, Eq. 21):

\[\begin{split} M = \begin{bmatrix} \mu & 0 & 0 & 0 & \mu x_{M3} & -\mu x_{M2} \\ 0 & \mu & 0 & -\mu x_{M3} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \mu & \mu x_{M2} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -\mu x_{M3} & \mu x_{M2} & i_{22} + i_{33} & 0 & 0 \\ \mu x_{M3} & 0 & 0 & 0 & i_{22} & -i_{23} \\ -\mu x_{M2} & 0 & 0 & 0 & -i_{23} & i_{33} \end{bmatrix}, \end{split}\]

with \(\mu = \langle\langle \rho \rangle\rangle\) the mass per unit length, \(x_{M\alpha} = \langle\langle \rho\, x_\alpha \rangle\rangle / \mu\) the mass center, \(i_{22} = \langle\langle \rho\, x_3^2 \rangle\rangle\), \(i_{33} = \langle\langle \rho\, x_2^2 \rangle\rangle\) and \(i_{23} = \langle\langle \rho\, x_2 x_3 \rangle\rangle\).

Shell engine#

The shell engine models a thin-walled beam by its cross-section contour. Its SG is a chain of line elements along the walls. Each wall segment is a plate model, and the beam law is homogenized over the contour, as in the thin-walled MSG model of Deo and Yu (2020). The layup of each wall is a 1-D SG through its thickness, whose homogenization gives the plate law of the wall: the \(A\), \(B\) and \(D\) blocks and, for the refined route, the shear block \(G\) of Yu et al. (2003).

The classical route gives the Euler-Bernoulli \(4\times4\) with Kirchhoff-Love walls and \(C^1\) Hermite elements, as in Deo and Yu (2020). The refined route uses the Reissner-Mindlin \(8\times8\) of each wall and Reissner-Mindlin shell elements (Bagla et al. 2025a). Its shell strains \(\Gamma_s\), the six plate strains and the two transverse shear strains of the wall, are written in terms of the beam strains and the shell fluctuations \(w_s = [w_1\ w_2\ w_3\ \omega_1\ \omega_2]^T\) (Bagla et al. 2025a, Eqs. 14 and 15):

\[ \Gamma_s = \Gamma_\varepsilon \bar\varepsilon + \Gamma_h w_s + \Gamma_l w_s' , \]

with shell forms of the three operators and \(\omega_1, \omega_2\) the fluctuations of the shell rotations (not the SG size \(\omega\)). The first-order beam step of Yu (2025, Sec. 6.3), applied to the contour, then gives the Timoshenko \(6\times6\).

Dehomogenization#

The macroscopic analysis gives the macro strains \(\bar\varepsilon\), or the forces, which the compliance \(\bar C^{-1}\) turns into strains. Then (Yu 2025, Sec. 7.3)

\[\begin{split} \begin{aligned} w &= S V_0\, \bar\varepsilon, \\ \Gamma &= \left(\Gamma_h S V_0 + \Gamma_\varepsilon\right) \bar\varepsilon, \\ \sigma &= C\, \Gamma , \end{aligned} \end{split}\]

and the local displacement is that of the macroscopic model, with its rotations, plus \(\epsilon w_i\).

For the Timoshenko beam, the six Timoshenko strains give \(\bar\varepsilon\) and \(\bar\varepsilon'\) through \(\bar\varepsilon = \bar\varepsilon_t + Q\gamma'\) and the beam equilibrium equations (Yu et al. 2012, Eqs. 112 to 116). The fluctuation keeps its first-order term, for a prismatic beam without distributed loads (Yu et al. 2012, Eq. 111)

\[ w = S \left( V_0\, \bar\varepsilon + V_{1S}\, \bar\varepsilon' \right), \]

and the strain split with \(w\) and \(w'\) gives the local strain.

The shell engine recovers on its refined route in two steps. The beam strains and the contour fluctuations give the six shell strains and the two transverse shear strains of every wall element. The 1-D SG of each wall then turns the six strains and their gradients along the span and the contour into the in-plane 3-D strains and stresses at any depth (Yu et al. 2003, Eq. 63). At first order \(\sigma_{33}\) is not available (Yu 2005, Sec. 5), and the shell engine does not recover the transverse shear stresses.
